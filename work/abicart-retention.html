A retention engine that took churn down thirty per cent

The same three years at Abicart, on the other half of the job. Acquisition was buying merchants; this was about keeping them, and it turned out to be the cheaper of the two.

The situation

On a subscription platform, churn is the number that decides whether acquisition is an investment or a treadmill. Abicart had the data to see churn coming and no system that looked at it.

The behaviour that predicts a merchant leaving happens weeks before they leave, and it sits in product usage rather than in the CRM.

What I changed

  • One view of the merchant. I joined product behaviour from the warehouse in SQL to analytics in GA4 and to the CRM record in HubSpot, so the same merchant was one person in all three rather than three separate rows.
  • Intervene on behaviour, not on the calendar. The triggers were built on what a merchant had stopped doing, which is the signal that arrives early, rather than on a renewal date, which arrives too late to act on.
  • Fix the funnel, not only the message. A good share of the saving came from removing the steps where merchants fell out, which is cheaper than winning them back afterwards.

What moved

Churn came down 30%, and the funnel improvements behind it saved $0.8M a year.

The part worth naming is that the saving is annual and compounding, while an acquisition campaign has to be bought again every month. This work cost a fraction of the paid budget and returned more of it.

What I would do differently

I would put a number on the merchants the system saved that were never going to leave. A retention programme that contacts everyone at risk will always claim the ones who were fine, and the honest version of this result needs a holdout the same way a paid campaign does.
