The glass I never checked

27 July 2026

At nineteen I wrote an essay explaining that people believe things which are not true.

I opened it with a wine glass. There is a company called Riedel, I told my readers, which sells the idea that every grape deserves its own shape of crystal, and millions of drinkers have been persuaded their wine tastes better from the correct one. Then the punchline. Run a proper blind test and the difference vanishes. Absolutely zero. Marketing, I wrote, makes wine taste better.

I had not read a single study. I had read a book that told me this, I liked the way it sounded, and I passed it along with the confidence of a man who had checked. This year I checked.

The studies do not say what I said they say

Start with the two papers usually held up as the debunking. Jeannine Delwiche and Marcia Pelchat, at the Monell Chemical Senses Center, concluded in print that the glass "does have a limited, but subtle, impact upon the olfactory experience of wines." Thomas Hummel and colleagues, testing 181 people, concluded that "the shape of glasses seems to influence the perception of wine odors." Both of those are the opposite of nothing. Two research groups outside the English-speaking literature, one in Spain in 2008 and one at Northwest A&F University in China in 2023, found the same direction of effect, the Chinese study using a design built specifically to separate the glass's volume from its shape.

There is physics underneath it. In 2015 a Japanese team imaged the vapour above a wine glass and found a ring of concentrated ethanol hovering at the rim, with a lower-alcohol pocket in the middle, a structure that depends on the shape of the bowl. Other work has measured how much alcohol simply evaporates away while you sit there, and found the headspace of the glass is one of the things that governs it.

The honest summary is conditional rather than triumphant. Charles Spence, who has reviewed this literature more carefully than anyone, puts it roughly this way: when a drinker can see and handle the glass, its size, shape, weight and colour all shift how they rate what is inside it, and when the taster genuinely cannot tell which glass they are working from, the glassware seems to make little difference. His conclusion is that the influence of the wine glass is "more psychological than physico-chemical in origin."

Which is close to what I was arguing. But it is not what I wrote. I wrote that science had proved there was nothing there, because that was the cleaner sentence, and because the book I was copying had written it that way first. An essay about people repeating convenient stories, opening with a convenient story I had repeated.

The best detail I found is one I would have killed for at nineteen, and it cuts against the company rather than the science. Pelchat told an interviewer in 2014 that when Riedel visited the lab, they asked whether he would like to fund the research. She remembered the answer as: "Yes, as long as you don't find anything negative about the glass." She declined. Riedel did not respond to the publication's request for comment, though another scientist present confirmed the remark was made.

The fact I should have used

If I had wanted to show that expert palates are less reliable than they look, the evidence was sitting there, and it is devastating.

Robert Hodgson spent four seasons, from 2005 to 2008, auditing the judging at the California State Fair wine competition, the oldest commercial wine competition in North America. Each panel of four judges received a flight of thirty wines, seeded with triplicate pours from the same bottle, scattered so nobody could spot them. Between sixty-five and seventy judges were tested each year. Only about one in ten could reproduce their own score on the same wine, in the same sitting, within a medal.

That is the real story, and it is better than mine. It does not need a glass company to be lying. It needs only for human beings to be human, and for a whole industry to be built on the assumption that they are not.

I had the sign backwards

The underlying mechanism I was gesturing at is real, and it survived the last decade better than most things in its field. When Hilke Plassmann and colleagues told people a wine cost ninety dollars rather than ten, the drinkers reported more pleasure and their brains showed it, and that basic result has been reproduced by the same lab with tighter methods, by an independent lab in a messy real-world setting, and in pain rather than taste. A precise little finding travels with it: price moves how much you enjoy something, not how intense you find it. You taste the same thing. You like it differently. In one striking medical version, Parkinson's patients given an identical saline injection improved twice as much when told it cost fifteen hundred dollars rather than one hundred.

But here is where nineteen-year-old me had it exactly backwards. I wrote that if we think expensive wine is better, it is. That is the weak half of the effect. The strong half is the other one.

In the tightest version of the price study, the expensive cue barely beat tasting the wine with no price at all, while the cheap cue collapsed the rating. In the famous energy-drink experiment, people who paid full price solved about as many puzzles as people given no drink whatsoever, while the people who got a discount solved far fewer. Cheapness degrades the experience much more powerfully than expensiveness improves it.

So the useful sentence is not "belief makes it better." It is "doubt makes it worse." Nine years of running paid acquisition have taught me that this is the more actionable of the two by a wide margin, and I had it upside down in print.

The literature had a problem of its own

I also wrote, in that essay, that granola is not healthy and that consumers decided it was. The idea I was leaning on, that a health claim on the front of a packet makes people misjudge what is inside, came largely out of Brian Wansink's food lab at Cornell.

In September 2018 a Cornell faculty committee found that Wansink had committed academic misconduct, including misreporting of research data and problematic statistical techniques. He resigned the following year. Retraction Watch counts eighteen retractions. Internal emails obtained under a records request include him telling a colleague that a p-value "should be lower" and that "if you can get the data, and it needs some tweeking, it would be good to get that one value below .05." He has always denied fraud.

Fairness cuts both ways here, so: his most famous result, the self-refilling soup bowl, was properly replicated in 2024 and held up at roughly half the original size, and the health-halo idea survives in independent work. My granola paragraph was not wrong. It was just resting on a shelf I had never inspected.

The same decade did worse to the honesty research I would have cited without hesitation. A celebrated 2012 paper claiming that signing a form at the top makes people more truthful was retracted in 2021 after independent analysts showed the field data had been fabricated; those analysts said plainly it was impossible to tell from the data who had done it. Duke investigated its author, Dan Ariely, for three years, and in a statement Duke approved he wrote that the committee "found no evidence to support claims that I falsified data or knowingly used falsified data," while determining that he "should have done more to prevent faulty data from being published." Separately, and before any of the fraud surfaced, the original authors themselves published a large preregistered replication reporting that the effect does not exist.

Step back far enough and the pattern is not about any one person. When 270 researchers re-ran 100 psychology studies, 97 percent of the originals had been statistically significant and 36 percent of the replications were. The two showcase results about unconscious influence, professor priming and ego depletion, both dissolved under multi-lab testing.

I could not have known any of this in January 2017. Most of it had not happened yet. But I did not ask, either, and the asking is the part I control.

Then I got the job

The deepest correction did not come from a scandal. It came from doing the work.

My essay had a simple engine: people adopt a story, and the story becomes true for them, and that is how brands are built. The empirical marketing literature says the engine runs the other way. Bird, Channon and Ehrenberg showed in 1970, and researchers reconfirmed across dozens of fresh datasets four decades later, that brand image follows brand usage rather than causing it. People buy the thing first. The beliefs rearrange themselves afterwards to fit. Byron Sharp's gloss is that consumers may simply be parroting back the advertising rather than believing anything at all.

What actually grows a brand, on that evidence, is unglamorous: more buyers rather than more devoted ones, being easy to recall and easy to find, being recognisable rather than meaningfully different. None of it requires anybody to believe a story.

That is not the whole picture, and I would not want it to be. Les Binet and Peter Field, working through hundreds of documented campaigns, find that emotional advertising roughly doubles profitability against rational advertising, and that brand-building and short-term activation are separate machines that need separate budgets. Story is not dead. It is just a delivery mechanism rather than the engine, and the nineteen-year-old had it as the engine.

The part I got right

There is one section of that old essay I will defend without qualification, and it is the only one I thought up myself.

I wrote that Facebook's business model had quietly become your time. That once it moved advertising into the feed on small screens, it discovered people would simply absorb it, and that the company would then work every day to make the feed harder to put down. I compared it to the cigarette industry and I used the phrase "selling you addiction."

That was January 2017. Sean Parker's confession about exploiting a vulnerability in human psychology came in November of that year. Chamath Palihapitiya's came in December. Scott Galloway's book came in 2018, and Facebook's own former director of monetisation testified to Congress in 2020. What I had available was Tristan Harris's essay from May 2016 and Tim Wu's book from that October. I got there early, and I have no interest in pretending otherwise.

What has come out since supports the core of it. Documents unsealed in 2025 describe an internal Meta study finding that a week away from Facebook lowered depression, anxiety and loneliness, and the company's own 2019 research estimating that about three percent of American users had problematic use.

And yet. The scandal that seemed to prove everything, Cambridge Analytica, largely deflated on inspection: the regulator that seized the servers concluded the company was using well recognised processes and commonly available technology, and that its most famous boast may have been an exaggeration. Cambridge Analytica's real product was a story it sold to campaigns, which the rest of us then sold to ourselves. That is the Riedel glass again, at the scale of democracies.

The science on teenagers is genuinely unsettled. One study of more than three hundred thousand adolescents found technology use explained under half a percent of variation in wellbeing, less than wearing glasses. The National Academies declined in 2024 to endorse population-level causation. Reviewing Jonathan Haidt's book, Candice Odgers called him "a gifted storyteller, but his tale is currently one searching for evidence." Meanwhile controlled deactivation trials do find small real effects, concentrated in young women.

I notice that Odgers reached for exactly my vocabulary to make her criticism. A gifted storyteller whose tale is searching for evidence is a fair description of the essay I wrote at nineteen.

What I would tell him

I used to think the job was finding a story people would believe. Nine years in a trade where I am paid to move numbers has taught me it is the reverse. The story is easy. Everyone can do the story. The discipline is auditing the story you personally find easiest to believe, because that is the one nobody is going to audit for you, and because being wrong at volume is expensive in a way being wrong in a blog post is not.

The essay I wrote at nineteen was mostly borrowed, and the parts I borrowed were the parts that were wrong. The part I worked out myself, sitting in an engineering college in Dhanbad with no particular authority to have an opinion, is the part that held up.

I am keeping that lesson and retiring the essay.
