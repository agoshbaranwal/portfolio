Doubt is louder than belief

27 July 2026

Marketing Behavioural science

Four expert judges sit down at the California State Fair to a flight of thirty wines. Nobody tells them that three of those glasses hold the same wine, poured from the same bottle, scattered through the flight so that no one can spot the trick.

Robert Hodgson ran that test for four seasons, on sixty-five to seventy judges a year. Only about one judge in ten could give the same wine the same score twice in a single sitting.

You already know what you want to do with that fact. Experts are frauds. Taste is a con. It is all just marketing.

Hold on. That conclusion is fun, it is popular, and it is wrong. What is actually going on is stranger, better evidenced, and far more useful to anyone who makes or sells anything.

the short version

  • What people expect really does change what they experience. That part is well established.
  • But it works far harder downwards than upwards.
  • Telling someone your product is good adds very little. Letting them suspect it is cheap takes away a lot.
  • So the money is in removing reasons to doubt, not in adding reasons to believe.

The glass that was supposed to settle it

The Riedel company sells the idea that every grape deserves its own shape of crystal. For years the standard reply has been that blind testing shows no difference whatsoever, and that the whole business is theatre.

Go and read the papers people cite for that and you find the opposite written in them. Jeannine Delwiche and Marcia Pelchat concluded that the glass "does have a limited, but subtle, impact upon the olfactory experience of wines." Thomas Hummel and colleagues, testing 181 people, concluded that "the shape of glasses seems to influence the perception of wine odors." Two groups far outside that conversation, one in Spain and one in China, found the same direction of effect, the second using a design built to separate the glass's volume from its shape.

There is even physics under it. A Japanese team photographed the vapour above a wine glass and found a ring of concentrated ethanol hanging at the rim with a weaker pocket in the middle, a shape that depends on the bowl.

So what is the honest answer? Conditional. Charles Spence, who has read this literature more carefully than anyone, puts it roughly this way. When you can see and hold the glass, its shape and size and weight and colour all move how you rate what is in it. When you genuinely cannot tell which glass you are drinking from, the glass stops mattering much. His verdict is that the effect is "more psychological than physico-chemical in origin."

Which is to say the sceptics were roughly right about the mechanism and completely wrong about the evidence. They had a clean line and they never checked it.

My favourite detail cuts the other way. Pelchat has said that when Riedel visited the lab, they asked if he wanted to fund the research. She remembered his answer as yes, as long as they did not find anything negative about the glass. She turned it down.

The price tag is doing something real

Give people the same wine twice. Tell them once that it costs ninety dollars and once that it costs ten. They report more pleasure from the expensive one, and the reward circuitry in their brain agrees with them. That study has since been reproduced by the original lab with tighter methods, by an independent lab in a messy real world setting, and in pain rather than taste.

One detail in it is worth memorising, because almost nobody quotes it. Price moves how much you enjoy something. It does not move how intense you find it. You are tasting the same thing. You are liking it differently.

This is not only about dinner. Parkinson's patients given an identical saline injection improved about twice as much when they were told it was the fifteen hundred dollar drug rather than the one hundred dollar one. Same needle, same saltwater. The only thing that changed was what they were told.

Now the part nobody quotes

Here is where the story everyone tells falls apart, and where this becomes worth your time.

In the tightest version of the price experiment, the expensive label barely beat drinking the wine with no price attached at all. The cheap label, though, sent the rating off a cliff.

The same asymmetry turns up away from wine. In a well known experiment, people who paid full price for an energy drink solved about as many puzzles afterwards as people who were given no drink at all. The people who got the same drink at a discount solved noticeably fewer.

Read those two results together and you get something that is not the usual lesson at all.

Expectation is a weak lever for lifting things up, and a powerful one for pulling them down.

Telling someone your product is excellent buys you close to nothing over saying nothing. Letting someone suspect it is cheap costs you a great deal. Belief barely builds. Doubt demolishes.

What this means if you sell anything

Most marketing advice is about installing a belief. Tell a better story, find your why, make them feel something. The evidence says the returns on that are real but modest, and that the returns on removing doubt are enormous.

Look at a discount properly. It does not simply cost you margin. It quietly informs the buyer that the thing is worth less, and then they experience it as worth less. You have paid money to make your own product worse.

The same goes for every careless signal around the thing you sell:

  • a cheap looking checkout page
  • a site that takes too long to load
  • a support reply that reads like nobody cared
  • a product photo that looks like a stock photo

None of these are the product. All of them tell the buyer to expect less, and people do what they are told.

I buy attention for a living, and this is the finding I think about most. It is easy to spend a quarter trying to make people believe something. It is usually faster to go and find the three places where you are giving them a reason to doubt.

Belief follows behaviour, not the other way round

There is a deeper reversal underneath all this, and it was published in 1970.

Bird, Channon and Ehrenberg showed, and researchers reconfirmed across dozens of fresh datasets decades later, that brand image follows brand usage rather than causing it. People do not fall in love and then buy. They buy, and then their opinions rearrange themselves to fit what they already do. Byron Sharp's blunt version is that when customers describe a brand warmly they may simply be parroting back the advertising.

So belief is mostly not what drives the buying. It is what follows it. That is why trying to install a belief is such slow work, and why clearing away doubt is so much faster.

Be suspicious of the version you enjoy

One caution, because this field has earned it.

A good deal of the popular science of persuasion has not survived inspection. When 270 researchers re-ran 100 psychology studies, ninety-seven of the originals had been statistically significant and thirty-six of the repeats were. A celebrated paper claiming people are more honest when they sign a form at the top was retracted after analysts showed its data had been fabricated. The Cornell food researcher behind a generation of viral eating studies was found by a faculty committee to have committed academic misconduct, and has eighteen retractions to his name.

The correct response is not to swing to the other extreme and decide that all of it is nonsense. That is the same lazy move as deciding all wine expertise is a con. His most famous result, the self refilling soup bowl, was carefully repeated in 2024 and it held up at roughly half the original size. A real effect that turns out smaller than advertised is the normal outcome, not a debunking.

Which gives you a rule worth more than any single study here. The claim you should check hardest is the one that flatters you. If a finding makes you feel clever for seeing through everybody, that is precisely when you have stopped checking.

So what do you actually do

Those judges in California were not doing anything dishonest, and they were not bad at their jobs. They had thirty glasses in front of them, they were tired, and the room was full of small hints about what they were drinking. You do the same thing when you pick a restaurant or choose a phone. Everybody does.

So stop treating it as a scandal and start treating it as something you can plan for.

Here is the practical version. Walk through everything a customer sees before they pay you:

  • the pricing page
  • the packaging
  • the checkout
  • the first email they get from you

Find the parts that quietly say this is cheap, or nobody here is paying attention. Fix those before you spend another month polishing your story.

You will not talk anyone into loving what you made. You can very easily talk them out of it, and most of the time you will do it by accident.
