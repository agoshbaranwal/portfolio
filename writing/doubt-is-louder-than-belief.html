Love is a lagging indicator

27 July 2026

Marketing Behavioural science

Four expert judges sit down at the California State Fair to a flight of thirty wines. Nobody tells them that three of those glasses hold the same wine, poured from the same bottle and scattered through the flight so the repeat is invisible.

Robert Hodgson ran that audit for four seasons, on sixty-five to seventy judges a year. About one judge in ten could score the same wine the same way twice in a single sitting. Roughly the same proportion, on some occasion, gave the identical wine anything from bronze to gold.

The comfortable conclusion is that expertise is theatre and the category runs on marketing. That conclusion is popular, it flatters whoever reaches it, and it will cost you money, because it points at the wrong strategy.

The useful question is not whether expectation moves people. It plainly does. The useful question is what kind of input expectation is, where it sits in the sequence, and what that implies about where the budget should go.

the short version

  • Expectation is a genuine input to experienced value, not a distortion of it. The frame is part of the product.
  • Expertise does not protect a buyer from framing. It amplifies the effect.
  • Attitude mostly follows behaviour. Preference sits downstream of purchase, not upstream of it.
  • So growth is a distribution and memory problem far more than a persuasion problem.
  • Love is a lagging indicator. Your job is the leading ones.

Expectation is an input, not an illusion

Give people the same wine twice, once described as costing ninety dollars and once as costing ten. Reported pleasure rises with the price, and the reward circuitry in the brain moves with it. That result has been reproduced by the original lab with tighter methods, by an independent lab in a messy field setting, and in pain rather than taste.

The commercially important detail is a dissociation almost nobody quotes. Price moved how much people enjoyed the wine. It did not move how intense they judged it to be. Sensory discrimination stayed intact. Hedonic value moved. Those are two different quantities, and only one of them appears on your product specification.

It travels beyond self-report. Parkinson's patients given an identical saline injection improved roughly twice as much on motor scores when they were told it was the fifteen hundred dollar drug rather than the one hundred dollar one. Same needle, same saltwater, different measured motor function.

The frame is not commentary on the product. It is a component of the product, and it shows up in the same measurements.

That collapses a hierarchy most organisations run on, in which the product is the real thing and the marketing is a description of it. Your price, your name, your packaging and the room the thing gets used in are not wrapped around the value. On the evidence, they are inside it.

Expertise does not protect the buyer. It amplifies.

The reflex is to assume this works on amateurs and washes off anyone who knows the category. The evidence points the other way.

In a study of 168 tasters, a white wine dyed to look like rosé was judged much more similar to a real rosé than to the identical undyed white. The people with tasting experience were moved more than the beginners, not less. A separate group running three experiments in a tasting laboratory served several identical wines within each flight, and nobody detected the repeats. Their conclusion was that experienced tasters express stronger opinions and adapt their evaluations more strongly after being given manipulative information.

Before writing off expertise entirely, note the corrective: in dark glasses with a sensible task, experts and novices both sort reds from whites by smell well above chance. The knowledge is real. It is simply not armour. Expertise supplies richer mental schemas, and a richer schema gives an incoming cue more to attach itself to.

If you sell to sophisticated buyers, to procurement teams, clinicians, engineers or finance committees, this is the finding worth sitting with. The belief that your buyer is too senior to be moved by framing is itself the least evidenced belief in the room.

Now resist the tidy law

In the tightest laboratory version of the price study, the expensive cue barely beat tasting with no price at all, while the cheap cue collapsed the rating. It is tempting to leave with a neat asymmetry: expectation lifts weakly and drops hard.

Then read the field study. In Basel, 140 people tasted three Italian reds. Deceptively raising the price of a cheap Montepulciano from ten francs to forty lifted its ratings significantly, and the up-priced cheap wine then outscored a genuinely more expensive wine sold at its real price. Deceptively cutting the price of a ninety-eight point Tuscan did nothing measurable.

Same manipulation. Opposite asymmetry. And in blind conditions with no price present at all, across roughly six thousand tasting observations, non-experts rate more expensive wines slightly worse rather than better.

So the defensible position is narrower than the one usually sold. Expectation reliably changes experienced value. It does not reliably change it in one direction. Direction depends on the reference point the buyer walked in with. Anyone offering you a one-line law here is selling you something, and the first section of this piece is where you should have been most suspicious of me.

The reversal that reorganises everything

There is a finding from 1970 that does more damage to conventional marketing planning than anything above.

Bird, Channon and Ehrenberg showed that the proportion of people expressing any given attitude about a brand depends systematically on how recently and how often they have bought it. It was retested in 2012 across forty-five fresh datasets and held. Attitude is a readout of behaviour, not its cause. Byron Sharp's blunt gloss is that when customers describe a brand warmly they may simply be parroting back the advertising.

Set that against the funnel every marketer is taught. Awareness, consideration, preference, purchase, loyalty. At least two of those arrows run backwards. People buy for reasons of availability, habit and convenience, and then assemble the attitude that makes their own behaviour look coherent.

Which means the thing most brand strategy is trying to manufacture, a preference that precedes and produces the purchase, is largely an output being mistaken for an input.

Which makes growth a distribution problem

The empirical marketing literature has been saying this for decades, with numbers you can check.

Take UK washing powder. Persil held twenty-two percent share on forty-one percent penetration, at 3.9 purchases per buyer. Ariel, fourteen percent share, twenty-six percent penetration, 3.9 purchases. Bold, ten percent share, nineteen percent penetration, 3.8 purchases. Loyalty is effectively flat across all three. Every point of share difference is penetration.

That pattern is a law, not an anecdote. Small brands get punished twice, with fewer buyers who also buy slightly less often. Independent corroboration keeps arriving from different directions:

  • Across 880 documented effectiveness cases, eighty-two percent of growth was penetration driven. Two percent came from loyalty.
  • A study of roughly one hundred thousand shoppers found leading brands run at least four times the category average penetration, while loyalty is similar and generally low for everyone, and top brands still churn close to half their buyers a year.
  • Marketing's Pareto law is empirically nearer sixty-twenty than eighty-twenty, and about eighty percent of buyers are extremely light. This year's top twenty percent deliver only around forty-five percent of volume next year, because heavy buyers regress.

The strategic consequence is unglamorous. Growth comes from more buyers, most of them light, most of them indifferent, most of whom will not think about you between purchases. A loyalty programme aimed at your best customers is aimed at precisely the group most likely to regress on its own.

And your instruments are flattering you

One more result, because it is the one that has changed how I spend money.

eBay switched off paid search advertising across sixty-eight US markets and compared the outcome against matched periods. Brand keyword ads produced no measurable short-term benefit. Returns overall were a fraction of what the conventional non-experimental analysis had reported.

Those ads were intercepting demand that already existed and booking it as demand created. If you have never run a geographic holdout or a proper incrementality test, you do not know your incremental return. You know your attribution, which is a different and considerably friendlier number.

So how do you actually make someone love your product

Put the pieces in order and the answer stops being a slogan.

Preference is downstream. Experience is partly constructed from the frame. Susceptibility rises rather than falls with expertise. Growth arrives through reach. What follows is a sequence, and the order matters more than any individual item in it.

  • Buy availability before belief. Be easy to recall in the moment a category need appears, and easy to buy once recalled. Nobody develops an attachment to something they never use, and attitude will not arrive before the behaviour it is a readout of.
  • Staff the frame like product. Price, name, packaging, the first screen and the physical context are not decoration around the value. They enter the measurement. Treat that work with the seriousness you give a feature release.
  • Defend coherence over amplification. Because susceptibility runs both ways and depends on reference points, a single contradicting cue can cost more than several confirming ones earn. A cheap looking checkout, a slow page or a careless support reply is not a cosmetic issue. It is a downward revision of the frame you paid to build.
  • Refuse to grade your own homework. Hold out a region, run the test, accept the smaller honest number. Everything above is only actionable if your measurement can tell interception from creation.

Do those four things at scale, for long enough, and affection shows up. Not because you convinced anyone, but because you made using the product easy and then never contradicted the story the product was already telling.

The takeaway

People genuinely do love products. The question was never whether that affection exists. It is where it sits in the sequence.

It is not the cause of the purchase you are trying to win this quarter. It is the residue of purchases you already made easy, inside a frame that never argued with itself. Which is why it cannot be bought directly, and why almost every attempt to buy it directly shows up as a campaign that moved sentiment and not sales.

Love is a lagging indicator. Your job is the leading ones.
